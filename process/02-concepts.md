# 02 · Thirty-one concepts

Each concept has: **hook** · **verb** (and why the verb is the meaning) · **first 10 seconds** (before / after the first
touch, with / without sound) · **new?** (claim + closest prior art from web search) · **fidelity** (what code renders
near-physically, what it has to fake).

Territories: art (1–6, 30), simulation (7–12), wonder/data (13–14), sound/music (8, 9, 15–17), language (18–20),
games (21–23, 31), tools (24–25), story (3, 26–27), humour (21, 22, 24, 28, 29).

---

## 1 · AS HANGS — architecture / physics
**Hook.** Pull a hanging chain down, and a stone cathedral rises in the mirror above it.

**Verb: pull down.** Robert Hooke, 1675: *"As hangs the flexible line, so but inverted will stand the rigid arch."* A chain
under its own weight is in pure tension; inverted, the same curve is in pure compression. Pulling a point down adds
weight there (Gaudí hung sacks of birdshot on his strings), and in the inverted world that weight is a spire, carried by
exactly the arch that can carry it. You make something stand by letting it hang. The verb *is* the theorem.

**First 10 s.** *Before touch:* a horizon splits the screen. Below, in a lamp-lit plaster workshop, an iron chain sways
between two ceiling hooks. Above, mirrored exactly, a pale stone arch stands on a plain in late sun, throwing a long shadow;
the chain's small sways make the shadow breathe. *After touch:* you grab the chain and pull; it kinks to your finger and,
above, the arch heaves upward into a pointed arch with a spire where your finger is. Let go: a small sack stays hanging,
and the spire stays. *Sound:* chain links clink (modal synthesis), the dry little workshop; above, wind; the stone settles with
a low knock. *Muted:* the mirror composition and the rising spire carry it.

**New?** The physics is 350 years old and form-finding is an architect's tool (Gaudí's models, Kangaroo, RhinoVAULT). The
new part is the experience: two mirrored worlds updating live, a spire rising from your pull, and walking into the vault you
made to hear its echo. *Prior art:* see 03.

**Fidelity.** *Exact:* chain-network equilibrium (dynamic relaxation), the inversion, sun and skylight on stone (path-traced
when still), link clinks (modal), the vault's reverberation (from its real volume and surfaces). *Fake:* stone surface detail,
masonry joints.

## 2 · OBSCURA — optics / art
**Hook.** Pierce the wall of a pitch-dark room and the world outside pours in, upside down.

**Verb: pierce.** Every image you have ever seen is light through a hole onto a surface. This room is an eye; each hole
lets another copy of the world in.

**First 10 s.** *Before:* black. A thin dusty beam from one pinhole; on the far wall, slowly, as if your eyes were adjusting,
a dim inverted moving image of a harbour at noon (sails, cloud shadows, glitter on water) falls across the plaster, a bed
and a chair, as in Abelardo Morell's photographs. *After:* a tap on any wall punches a hole (a knock, a new shaft of dust)
and a second inverted harbour overlaps the first. Dragging widens it: brighter, softer. *Sound:* the harbour leaks in through
the holes (gulls, rigging, water); more holes, more world. *Muted:* the inverted moving world on the walls.

**New?** Camera obscuras are tourist attractions; Morell's photographs are famous; pinhole widgets exist. A room you turn into a
camera yourself, from one hole to a lens to a plate that takes the first photograph, I haven't seen. *Prior art:* see 03.

**Fidelity.** *Exact:* the projection is the scene convolved with the aperture (blur, multiple copies, lens focus),
brightness ∝ hole area, crescents in an eclipse, a room lit only by its own projection. *Forgiving:* the image is dim and soft,
so a good procedural harbour reads as photographic. *Fake:* the harbour itself.

## 3 · STILL WARM — story / unease
**Hook.** A thermal camera in a house someone has just left. Everything they touched is still warm, and fading.

**Verb: look, and touch.** You pan through dark rooms reading heat, and every trace cools as you watch: looking is a race
against thermodynamics, and temperature tells time (a 45 °C mug was poured twenty minutes ago). Your own touches leave warm
fingerprints on the lens that fade at the same rate as theirs. You are a trace too.

**First 10 s.** *Before:* an ironbow false-colour kitchen: cold blue walls; a chair glowing amber; a mug white-hot with a plume;
two orange handprints on the table edge, already dimming; footprints across tiles to a door. Sensor grain, a temperature
readout. *After:* a warm fingerprint blooms where you touched and starts to fade; dragging pans to the next room. *Sound:* the
camera's fan, a fridge, a clock, a car pulling away outside. *Muted:* entirely visual.

**New?** Thermal vision is a game mode (Splinter Cell) and an art lens (Richard Mosse). A wordless story told only in residual
heat, where cooling dates every trace, I haven't seen. *Prior art:* see 03.

**Fidelity.** *Exact:* surface heat diffusion and Newton cooling. A thermal image *is* a smooth temperature field, so the model's
output is what a real camera shows; also plumes, emissivity (shiny metal reads cold), sensor noise, palettes. *Fake:* room
geometry (simple meshes suffice in thermal), the story.

## 4 · ARBORGLYPH — art / time
**Hook.** Carve your initials into a beech tree, then watch a hundred years stretch them.

**Verb: carve.** People carve trees to make something last. Trees don't lift a carving, they widen it, scar it and swallow it.
Wanting permanence meets the thing that actually lasts.

**First 10 s.** *Before:* a smooth grey beech trunk in dappled light, already carved at many ages: a crisp "A+J 2019", a fat
blurred heart from the 1950s, a scar you can barely read. *After:* dragging rasps pale wood through the bark. Release and time
runs: seasons flicker, your cut browns, callus rolls in, the letters fatten sideways. *Sound:* leaves, the knife, birds that change
with the decades. *Muted:* the carving and the time-lapse.

**New?** Optionally one shared tree that every visitor carves, each carving ageing in real time. *Prior art:* see 03.

**Fidelity.** *Exact enough:* radial growth, tangential stretch of a scar with circumference, callus closure; beech bark is
noise-friendly. *Fake:* the forest, a century of light.

## 5 · HOLD STILL — photography / art
**Hook.** A long exposure of a busy square where only still things exist. To appear in it, you have to stop.

**Verb: hold still.** In Daguerre's 1838 photograph of a crowded Paris boulevard, the street is empty except for one man who
stood still having his boots polished: the first person ever photographed. Presence is stillness.

**First 10 s.** *Before:* a silvered plate developing: buildings sharp, everything moving reduced to smears. *After:* your cursor is a
figure; moving, you're a streak; stopped, you burn into the plate second by second. *Sound:* the square's bustle fades the longer
you stay still. *Muted:* works.

**New?** Stillness installations and long-exposure apps exist; entering a photograph by stillness, I haven't seen. *Prior art:* see 03.

**Fidelity.** *Exact:* the time integral (the exposure), the daguerreotype's mirror plate flipping negative with angle. *Fake:* the
moving crowd (forgiving: they're blurs), the architecture.

## 6 · ETCH — deep time / material
**Hook.** Wipe acid across a slab of meteorite and the crystals of a dead planet's core appear.

**Verb: wipe.** The pattern is already in the metal; you reveal it. It grew while the core of a shattered planetesimal cooled by only
a few hundred to a few thousand degrees per million years, a slowness no laboratory can reproduce.

**First 10 s.** *Before:* a mirror-polished slab of iron with a fusion-crusted edge, catching the light. *After:* where you wipe, the
mirror dulls and the Widmanstätten lattice appears, bands at octahedral angles that shimmer differently as you tilt. *Sound:* acid hiss,
a faint ring. *Muted:* works.

**New?** Widmanstätten slices are museum staples; etching one yourself, I haven't seen. *Prior art:* see 03.

**Fidelity.** *Exact:* kamacite plates on the four {111} planes sectioned by the slab (the pattern is geometry), anisotropic reflection
of etched lamellae. *Fake:* inclusions.

## 7 · SLOW LIGHT — physics / wonder
**Hook.** Fire one pulse of light into a still life and watch it crawl, at a trillion frames a second.

**Verb: fire, then scrub.** You make the light, then hold time to see where it is.

**First 10 s.** *Before:* a dark tabletop: a glass of water, an apple, crumpled paper, a mirror. A small caption: "1 frame = 1
trillionth of a second". A bullet of light crosses slowly, splits at the glass, slows inside it, spreads through the apple as a
glow, finds the mirror and returns. *After:* you fire from where you tap; dragging scrubs time. *Sound:* light is silent; any sound
is design. *Muted:* works fully.

**New?** Femto-photography is real (MIT, 2011) and light-in-flight videos go viral; aiming the pulse yourself in a browser is rare.
*Prior art:* see 03.

**Fidelity.** *Exact:* time of flight (source → point → camera), single scattering in air and water, time-resolved diffusion in the
apple, light slowing to ⅔c in glass. *Approximate:* multi-bounce transients.

## 8 · THUNDER — sound / weather
**Hook.** Draw a bolt of lightning and hear the thunder its exact shape makes.

**Verb: strike.** Every bend you draw becomes part of the rumble. The shape is the sound.

**First 10 s.** *Before:* a dark plain under low cloud, distant flicker. *After:* you drag and a bolt forks down your stroke; the land
flashes white; silence while the sound travels; then the thunder: a crack from segments broadside to you, a long roll from segments
pointing away. *Sound:* the point. *Muted:* the flash, then a ring of "sound" crossing the land toward you.

**New?** Procedural thunder exists (Farnell); geometric thunder is 1980s acoustics research. Your drawing as the source, I haven't seen.
*Prior art:* see 03.

**Fidelity.** *Exact:* per-segment N-wave arrival times and amplitudes, air absorption, ground reflection, terrain echo. *Fake:* the cloud
volume, the channel's glow.

## 15 · 52 HZ — sound / loneliness
**Hook.** Call across an ocean in a voice nobody answers.

**Verb: call.** Pressing sings; how long you hold is how long you call. Since 1989 navy hydrophones have tracked a whale calling at
52 Hz, a frequency no other whale uses. Calling and waiting is the oldest form of loneliness, and here the physics decides
who hears you.

**First 10 s.** *Before:* a deep-blue cross-section of an ocean basin, thousands of kilometres wide and squashed into the screen:
the bright surface, the sound channel at ~1 km depth glowing faintly, the black floor. Far off, a low two-part call; much later
an answer from the other side, its rays curving through the channel. *After:* holding sends your call out as a fan of rays that
bend back and forth across the channel axis and refocus in convergence zones, crossing the basin in real (compressed) time.
The others go on answering each other. Nothing answers you. *Sound:* the calls, transposed into hearing; the ocean's low roar.
*Muted:* the fans of light-rays crossing the basin, yours unanswered.

**New?** The 52-hertz whale has a documentary and songs; SOFAR ray diagrams are oceanography textbook figures. A playable channel
where your call is ray-traced, I haven't seen. *Prior art:* see 03.

**Fidelity.** *Exact:* ray acoustics through a Munk sound-speed profile (channel trapping, convergence zones, travel times), spreading
and absorption. *Fake:* the whales (unseen), the call timbres (tonal sweeps are easy and honest).

## 16 · NOON — music / place
**Hook.** Every bell in a city rings at noon, and the speed of sound turns them into a canon you can walk through.

**Verb: stand somewhere.** Where you stand decides the music: near the cathedral it leads; at the edge of town the parish
bells arrive first and the great bell trails seconds behind. Somewhere there is a point where they all arrive together.

**First 10 s.** *Before:* an old city seen from above at 11:59:50, roofs and towers, gulls; the clock hand moving. *After:* at noon
every tower strikes; rings of sound expand from each tower at 343 m/s (drawn as faint ripples over the roofs) and you hear each
arrive in order of distance, quieter and duller the farther it came. Dragging moves your listening point and the canon
rearranges itself. *Sound:* the point. *Muted:* the expanding rings and a pulsing marker where they reach you.

**New?** Bells and spatial audio are common; a city-sized canon whose order is distance, I haven't seen. *Prior art:* see 03.

**Fidelity.** *Exact:* delays (distance/c), air absorption by distance, bell partials (modal synthesis), rooftop occlusion as
filtering. *Fake:* the city.

## 17 · STALACPIPE — music / deep time
**Hook.** A cave of stalactites you can play, where each note took ten thousand years to grow.

**Verb: tap.** Playing is touching deep time: a stalactite's pitch is its length, and its length is millennia of dripping.

**First 10 s.** *Before:* a cave lit by your headlamp, wet limestone glistening, drips plinking into a pool. *After:* a tap rings a
stalactite with a clear stony bell tone, and the chamber answers with a long, dark reverb; a ripple of light runs down the
formation. Longer formations ring lower. *Sound:* the point. *Muted:* weak (shimmering stone only).

**New?** Luray Caverns' Great Stalacpipe Organ (1956) is a real instrument; a virtual one with physical modes, I haven't seen.
*Prior art:* see 03.

**Fidelity.** *Exact:* modal synthesis of tapered stone rods, the cave's reverberation from its volume. *Fake:* cave geometry, wet
stone shading.

## 18 · ALEPH — language / history
**Hook.** Type your name, then drag it back 3,500 years until it is a row of little pictures.

**Verb: drag back in time.** Our letters began as drawings (A an ox's head, B a house, D a door, M water); going back un-abstracts
your name until you can see what it was made of.

**First 10 s.** *Before:* a caret blinking on a slab of pale stone. *After:* you type your name in clean Roman capitals; dragging left
slides through time and each letter morphs: Roman inscription, Greek, Phoenician on a potsherd, Proto-Sinaitic scratched on
turquoise-mine rock, until it's a row of little drawings: ox, house, snake, hand. The material changes with the era: chisel,
reed pen, ink on clay, scratch on sandstone. *Sound:* chisel taps, scratches. *Muted:* works fully.

**New?** "Your name in hieroglyphs" sites and alphabet-evolution charts are common; continuous morphing of your own name through
attested forms, I haven't seen. *Prior art:* see 03.

**Fidelity.** *Exact:* type, carving, ink and stone render perfectly. *Fake:* the historical glyph forms must be drawn by hand
(≈26 letters × 6 eras) and the in-betweens are interpolation.

**Fidelity.** *Exact:* type and strata. *Needs:* first-attestation dates for ~30k words (Wiktionary-derived, imperfect).

**New?** Moving-sofa visualisations exist as maths demos. *Prior art:* see 03.

**Fidelity.** *Exact:* rigid-body contact. *Fake:* nothing important (a top-down sofa is easy).

## 28 · MURPHY — humour / physics
**Hook.** Toast lands butter-side down because of the fundamental constants of the universe.

**Verb: nudge.** You push toast off the table again and again, and the universe answers the same way every time. Robert Matthews (1995):
toast tips off a table, rotates about half a turn before it reaches the floor, and lands butter-down. Table height is set by human
height, and human height is limited by the strength of chemical bonds against gravity. To see butter-up you'd need a 3 m table, and
to use one you'd need to be about 6 m tall, which the fine-structure constant won't allow.

**First 10 s.** *Before:* a slice of hot buttered toast at the edge of a kitchen table, butter glistening. *After:* nudge it and it tips,
tumbles in slow motion and lands butter-down with a wet slap. Try again: same. Flick it hard and it can survive (a real escape).
Then the table starts to grow. *Sound:* the slap. *Muted:* works.

**New?** Matthews' paper won an Ig Nobel in 1996; MythBusters tested it. *Prior art:* see 03.

**Fidelity.** *Exact:* tumbling rigid body off an edge, friction, slip. *Fake:* nothing.

## 31 · FREEFALL — body / game
**Hook.** Hold to dive. You'll find you're holding your breath.

**Verb: hold.** The press is the breath-hold: most people will hold their own breath without being asked. Calm (not moving) extends it;
past about 12 m your lungs compress, you become heavier than water, and you stop swimming and fall. Letting go is the ascent, and
the dangerous part is the last ten metres.

**First 10 s.** *Before:* just under a bright sea surface, light dancing, the sound of your breathing through the surface. *After:* holding
ducks you under; reds drain from the reef wall, ears click, the heart slows; at 12 m you stop finning and sink into blue. *Sound:*
heartbeat, ear clicks, silence. *Muted:* works.

**New?** Freediving games and neal.fun's Deep Sea exist. *Prior art:* see 03.

**Fidelity.** *Exact:* spectral attenuation with depth, light shafts, pressure. *Fake:* the reef wall.

# Added after the first research round: five game and humour concepts

The first 31 leaned toward contemplative art pieces, and the game and humour entries were one-joke ideas. These five aim for
games and comedy with depth (goals, mastery, escalation) that still meet the brief. The session's web-search budget was
spent by then, so their prior art comes from memory and directly fetched pages only.

## 32 · THERMAL — game / air
**Hook.** A glider with no engine: to stay up, you have to find air that rises, and you can't see it.

**Verb: circle.** You climb by turning in a tight circle inside an invisible column of warm air, the way hawks do. Rising by going
round, patience as altitude. The audio variometer (the beeping instrument every glider pilot knows) sings faster and higher as you climb.

**First 10 s.** *Before:* silence and wind; you glide over a sunlit valley, slowly sinking, the vario a low unhappy drone; ahead, a
hawk circles under a growing cumulus. *After:* steering toward the hawk, the vario starts chirping; banking into a circle, the
horizon wheels and the valley drops away as you climb. *Sound:* wind, the vario. *Muted:* the wheeling horizon and the ground falling away.

**New?** Gliding sims (Condor, Silent Wings) are full flight simulators with cockpits and instruments. An engine-less art-game where
the only verb is turning and the air is invisible but audible, I haven't seen. *Prior art:* no searches possible; known works only.

**Fidelity.** *Exact:* thermal plumes from sun-heated ground, glider polar (sink rate vs speed), the vario tone, cumulus bases at the
condensation level, the day's thermal cycle. *Hard:* volumetric cumulus at Shadertoy quality; terrain from altitude.

## 33 · CANUTE — game / impermanence
**Hook.** Build a sandcastle at the waterline, then defend it against a rising tide that always wins.

**Verb: dig and pile.** Every wall you raise is a bargain with the sea. King Canute commanded the tide to stop to prove to his
courtiers that he couldn't. Your score is how long it stands, and it always falls.

**First 10 s.** *Before:* a sandcastle at golden hour; a wave slides up, fizzes into the moat and drains back, darkening the sand.
*After:* dragging piles wet sand into a wall; dragging the other way digs a channel; the next wave hits your wall and a corner slumps.
*Sound:* surf, the hiss of foam, sand. *Muted:* works.

**New?** Beach-builder casual games exist; a sandcastle whose fate is decided by granular slumping and real wave run-up, with the tide
as the clock, I haven't seen. *Prior art:* no searches possible.

**Fidelity.** *Exact:* wet vs dry sand (angle of repose, darkening), shallow-water run-up, erosion. *Risk:* it reads as a fluid sandbox.

## 34 · WOBBLE — humour / maths
**Hook.** A wobbly café table can always be steadied by rotating it, and this page proves it to you on worse and worse ground.

**Verb: rotate.** The wobbly-table theorem: a four-legged table on continuous uneven ground always has a stable angle within 90°.
Don't fold a napkin under the leg; turn the table. Later grounds get absurd (a hillside, steps, a crater) and it still works.

**First 10 s.** *Before:* a café table on cobbles, an espresso trembling, the cup rattling on its saucer as the table rocks. *After:*
dragging rotates the table; the rocking changes, the rattle stops, the coffee goes still. *Sound:* the rattle. *Muted:* the trembling cup.

**New?** Numberphile's 2014 video made the theorem famous; an interactive with escalating ground, I haven't seen. *Prior art:* no
searches possible.

**Fidelity.** *Exact:* four-leg contact on a height field, rocking, the cup's rattle. *Fake:* nothing.

## 35 · TANGLE — humour / maths
**Hook.** Put a cord in a box, shake it for three seconds, and it ties itself into a knot, every time. The page names the knot.

**Verb: shake.** Raymer & Smith (PNAS, 2007) tumbled strings in a box: knots formed in about half of trials, more for longer strings.
Everyone's earphones do this. Shaking is the verb; the universe's tendency to tangle is the joke; the knot's name is the punchline.

**First 10 s.** *Before:* a long cord lying loose in a wooden box. *After:* dragging shakes the box; the cord tumbles; the box comes to
rest and the cord is tied in a trefoil, which the page names. Again: a figure-eight, a 5₂. *Sound:* cord rattling in the box. *Muted:* works.

**New?** Rope toys are common; spontaneous knotting with knot identification, I haven't seen. *Prior art:* no searches possible.

**Fidelity.** *Exact:* rope dynamics with self-collision, knot invariants (Alexander polynomial). *Risk:* self-collision stability.

## 36 · OVERHANG — game / maths / humour
**Hook.** Stack bricks off the edge of a table as far as they'll reach, and find out what infinity costs.

**Verb: stack.** With n bricks the classic stack reaches half the harmonic series, so you can reach any distance, but two brick-lengths
takes 31 bricks and ten takes 272 million. Paterson and Zwick (2006) showed cleverer stacks with counterweights do far better
(∝ n^⅓). Every brick you add is a little negotiation with the harmonic series.

**First 10 s.** *Before:* a stack of bricks on a table edge, the top one jutting out over a drop. *After:* dragging a brick onto the
stack, it teeters, settles or topples with a satisfying clatter; the reach is marked in brick-lengths. *Sound:* brick clacks. *Muted:* works.

**New?** The book-stacking problem is a famous puzzle; Paterson–Zwick stacks as a physical game, I haven't seen. *Prior art:* no searches possible.

**Fidelity.** *Exact:* rigid blocks with friction and toppling. *Fake:* nothing.
